Why the LP backfires: it cuts gain broadband, so it injects phase lag in the structural band (−5° at 2.4 Hz, −22° at 10 Hz). That lag (1) is an effective delay → worse RTHS tracking (J4 88→100%), and (2) erodes the 2.4 Hz mode margin — which is what actually binds your worst case → NCF 0.256→0.225. The 37 Hz mode it was meant to fix isn't even the limiting one at the nominal operating point.

The LP only earns its keep when the 37 Hz CSI mode is binding (heavy damage, or after an aggressive lead has pushed the crossover up to 37). So keep it as a damage‑triggered emergency roll‑off scheduled by your OSI margin, not a fixed performance filter.

A "specific higher‑level phase lead": yes — decisively

The first‑order lead is too blunt: it buys in‑band phase but pays with +17 dB of gain at 37 Hz, which is exactly what destabilizes the damaged plant. The fix is to decouple the two jobs with a higher‑order compensator — a notch at the CSI frequency + an in‑band lead:

- the notch removes loop gain only at ~37 Hz (−10 to −14 dB there), preserving in‑band phase (unlike the broadband LP);
- the lead supplies the in‑band phase that performance and the 37 Hz phase margin need.

notch@37 + lead(z3,p15) gives worst‑case NCF 0.320 (clears your 0.30 floor), stable across the entire damage bracket, J4 49%/17% — it recovers most of the aggressive lead's performance without its fragility. Push more in‑band lead (z2, z1.5) and J4 keeps falling (~5%) but NCF drops below 0.30 — there's a genuine performance–robustness frontier, with the knee right around notch@37 + lead(z3,p15).

This is precisely the 2‑pole/2‑zero family you already started (fem3_ncf4_2p2z.mat). Recommendation: optimize that structure (or notch+lead) with the min–max damage‑bracket criterion — not a first‑order lead.

Two caveats: center the notch at f_inst (the actuator −180° frequency, ~37 Hz) and make it wide enough to cover the damage drift 37→~42 Hz or schedule it (a sharp notch can ring — the smooth lead‑lag roll‑off at NCF 0.282 is a gentler alternative). And the residual ceiling on worst‑case NCF is set by the 2.4 Hz mode, which is a PID job — lower I — so co‑tune I with the compensator.

Bottom line

- Mechanism: LP = broadband gain cut (collaterally lags your in‑band response); notch = surgical gain cut at the CSI frequency (keeps your in‑band lead). That's why notch ≫ LP here.
- Don't add a fixed 25 Hz low‑pass — it makes both worse at your operating point.
- Do move to a higher‑order notch@37 + in‑band lead (2p2z), optimized worst‑case over the damage bracket, with the notch placed/sized at f_inst and the PID I co‑tuned.

Formulation C is the answer to your question. Schedule a=1 before damage, a→0 after, and because both endpoints are good designs (aggressive lead for healthy, robust notch+lead for damaged), you keep good performance in both states. Naively scaling the command down (A) restores stability but costs you ~43% J4 because it literally under‑drives the specimen amplitude.

Three conditions for it to be safe

1. You must schedule — no constant a is good for both (healthy needs 1, damaged needs ≤0.4).
2. Blend toward a robust controller, not toward "less command." A "turn the gain down" knob trades performance ~linearly and biases the displacement; blending between two designed controllers does not.
3. The transition is the danger. On the damaged plant, intermeill unstable (pole >1); only a≤0.4 is safe. pole is monotone in a (lower = safer) and lowering a is always safe when healthy — but if damage hits while you're at a=1, you're already unstable, so you must drop a decisively and fast off the OSI Method‑7 margin trigger, not c

Practical recommendation

- If you trust online detection: run formulation C, scheduled by the OSI margin — a=1 when margin is ample (15% J4), snap to a=0 when b̂ drops (17% J4, safe). This recovers ~3× healthy performance vs running the robust controller always.
- If you don't fully trust detection: just run the notch+lead always (J4 49% healthy / 17% damaged, both safe, no switching risk), and use the authority scalar only as an emergency brake.
