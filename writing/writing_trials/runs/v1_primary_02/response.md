# Purpose

Explain the physical mechanism suggested by laboratory observations while qualifying its dependence on transfer-system and specimen dynamics.

# Sequence

- Connect stiffness degradation to the suggested amplification mechanism.
- Explain the controller tuning under the initial stiffness condition.
- Trace stiffness loss to greater response under the same corrective action.
- Identify specimen mass and system interaction as relevant influences.
- Qualify the mechanism and stability-indicator performance across different dynamics.

# First draft

Laboratory observations suggest that stiffness degradation can promote response amplification through the interaction between the specimen and the transfer system. The controller is initially tuned to overcome the relatively high specimen stiffness while maintaining the required tracking performance. When the specimen loses stiffness, it becomes easier to move, so the same corrective control action is likely to produce greater overshoot and a larger displacement response than under the initial stiffness condition. The specimen mass also influences the extent of this response amplification. The interaction between the degraded specimen and the transfer system, including the controller, can eventually drive the system toward instability. However, this behavior is not universal because the dominant instability mechanism and the performance of stability indicators can vary substantially with the dynamics of both the transfer system and the specimen.

# Final paragraph

Laboratory observations suggest how specimen stiffness degradation can amplify the displacement response and increase the risk of instability. The controller is initially tuned to overcome the relatively high specimen stiffness while maintaining the required tracking performance. As the specimen loses stiffness, it becomes easier to move. The same corrective control action is therefore likely to produce greater overshoot and a larger displacement response than under the initial stiffness condition. The specimen mass also influences the extent of this response amplification. The interaction between the degraded specimen and the transfer system, including the controller, can eventually drive the system toward instability. This behavior is not universal. Both the dominant instability mechanism and the performance of stability indicators can vary substantially with the dynamics of the transfer system and the specimen.
