# First draft

Laboratory observations suggest that the gain amplification described above can arise from the changing response of the specimen as it loses stiffness. The controller is initially tuned to overcome the relatively high specimen stiffness and provide the required tracking performance. After stiffness degradation, the specimen becomes easier to move, and keeping the same corrective control action is likely to cause more overshoot and a larger displacement response than under the initial stiffness condition. The extent of this response amplification also depends on specimen mass. The interaction between the degraded specimen and the transfer system, including the controller, can eventually drive the system toward instability. This behavior is not universal because the dominant instability mechanism and stability-indicator performance can vary substantially with both transfer-system and specimen dynamics.

# Final paragraph

Laboratory observations suggest a physical explanation for this gain amplification. The controller is initially tuned to overcome the relatively high specimen stiffness and achieve the required tracking performance. As the specimen loses stiffness, it becomes easier to move. The same corrective control action is then likely to produce greater overshoot and a larger displacement response than under the initial stiffness condition. Specimen mass also influences the extent of this response amplification. The resulting interaction between the degraded specimen and the transfer system, including the controller, can eventually drive the system toward instability. However, this behavior is not universal. Both the dominant instability mechanism and the performance of stability indicators can vary substantially with the dynamics of the transfer system and specimen.
